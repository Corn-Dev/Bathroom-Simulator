# Long Form Core Guidance

Stable guidance for long form YouTube videos. This file is author-controlled and changes rarely.
Performance-based insights live separately in `long-form-insights.md`.

---

## The Fundamentals

**The intro must earn the next 30 seconds.**
Don't open with "hey guys welcome back." Open with the thing. Show the broken bug, the moment it worked, the decision you were stuck on. Give the viewer a reason to stay before you introduce yourself or the topic.

**Have a throughline.**
The video needs one central thread the viewer can follow, even if you cover multiple features or topics. "I built multiplayer" is not a throughline. "I spent two weeks trying to get players to see each other and here's what actually happened" is a throughline. The viewer should be able to answer "what was this video about?" in one sentence.

**Transitions need to feel connected.**
Don't just cut to the next topic. Bridge it. "That got the lobby working — but now I had a new problem..." is a transition. "Okay so next I worked on voice chat" is not.

**Vary the energy.**
Long form needs moments to breathe. High energy sections (something working, a funny bug, a reveal) need to be followed by something slower (explaining context, showing code, talking through a decision). If everything is high energy, nothing is.

**Show don't tell.**
Gameplay and screen recordings beat talking head explaining things. If you can show it, show it. If you have to explain it, keep it short and cut to the visual as fast as possible.

**End with something conclusive.**
The viewer should feel like the video is done, not just stopped. Tease what's next, but close the current arc first. "So that's where the game is now — next time I'm tackling X" works. Trailing off doesn't.

## Voice and Personality

**Your development journey is the differentiator.**
Generic feature showcases are forgettable. Your specific experience building this — the frustration, the wrong turns, the moment it clicked — is what makes someone subscribe. Lean into it.

**Talk like a person.**
The script is a guide, not a teleprompter. Write it the way you'd actually say it. If a sentence sounds weird when you read it out loud, rewrite it.

**The viewer is a gamer, not a developer.**
You can mention technical things, but always anchor them to what it means for the game. "I had to rewrite the networking layer" means nothing. "I had to rewrite the networking layer because players kept teleporting around the map" means something.

## Structure Patterns That Work

**Problem → Struggle → Solution**
Open with the problem (players can't see each other). Show the struggle (two days of debugging). Land on the solution (it works). This arc is reliable and relatable.

**Feature Showcase with Stakes**
Show the feature working. Then show what it took to get there, or what it unlocks for the game. Don't just demo — give it context.

**Decision Moment**
You were at a fork. Here are the options. Here's why you went the way you did. Here's what it means going forward. Works well as a section within a larger video.

---

## What to Avoid

- Opening with "in this video I'm going to show you..." — just show it
- Spending more than 60 seconds on setup before anything interesting happens
- Covering more than 3–4 major topics in one video — pick the best ones
- Ending on a cliffhanger with no payoff in the current video
- Over-explaining technical implementation when the visual tells the story
